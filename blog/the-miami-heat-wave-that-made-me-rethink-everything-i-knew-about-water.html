The Miami Heat Wave That Made Me Rethink Everything I Knew About Water

2026-07-11 · 5 min read · General

The Miami Heat Wave That Made Me Rethink Everything I Knew About Water

I collapsed on the sidewalk outside my apartment building on Brickell Avenue at 2:47 PM on a Tuesday in August. Not dramatically. Not like in the movies. I just sat down on the curb because my legs stopped working and I could not make them start again. A tourist walked past me wearing a "Miami Vice" tank top and took a photo. I did not care. I was too busy trying to remember my own name.

I am a hydration scientist. I have a PhD in exercise physiology from the University of Miami. I have published papers on sweat sodium concentration and electrolyte replacement strategies. I have consulted for three sports drink companies. And I had just dehydrated myself to the point of heat exhaustion because I thought I knew what I was doing.

Here is how it happened. I was training for the Miami Marathon — my fourth — and I was doing a 16-mile long run on the Rickenbacker Causeway. It was 94 degrees. The humidity was 78%. The heat index was 112. I had my handheld water bottle. I had my electrolyte tablets. I had my heart rate monitor. And I had a plan: drink to thirst, replace electrolytes, listen to my body.

The problem was that my body was lying to me.

At mile 8, I felt fine. My heart rate was 165, which is high but normal for me at that pace in heat. I was sipping water every mile. I was taking salt tabs every 45 minutes. I was checking the boxes. But what I did not realize was that my sweat rate was 2.8 liters per hour — nearly three times the average for a runner in moderate conditions. I was losing fluid faster than I was replacing it, and by the time my thirst mechanism caught up, I was already 2.5% dehydrated.

That is the critical threshold. At 2% dehydration, performance drops. At 3%, cognitive function declines. At 4%, you are in the danger zone. I hit 4.2% at mile 14. My heart rate spiked to 188. My vision narrowed. I started seeing spots. And then my legs just stopped.

I sat on that curb for twenty minutes. A cyclist stopped and gave me a bottle of water. I drank it slowly — too fast and I would have vomited. I called an Uber. The driver looked at me like I was insane when I told him I was a hydration scientist who had just dehydrated himself. He said, "That is like a chef who burns his own dinner." I said, "Exactly."

I spent the next three days in bed with a headache that felt like someone was hammering nails into my skull. Not a hangover headache. A dehydration headache. The kind that does not respond to ibuprofen because the problem is not inflammation — it is fluid volume. I drank 5 liters of water per day. I ate salty foods. I slept fourteen hours. And I thought about every piece of advice I had ever given.

"Drink to thirst." That was the standard recommendation. And it is fine for moderate exercise in moderate conditions. But in Miami in August, thirst is a lagging indicator. By the time you feel thirsty in 94-degree heat with 78% humidity, you are already behind. Your body is screaming for water because it is in crisis, not because it is being proactive.

I had been telling people to drink to thirst for years. And I had been wrong — at least for high-heat, high-humidity environments. The research supports this. Studies from the Korey Stringer Institute show that athletes in hot conditions consistently underestimate their fluid needs by 30-40% when relying on thirst alone. The mechanism works in cool weather. It fails in the tropics.

I changed my approach. I started weighing myself before and after runs — nude, to account for sweat-soaked clothing. I calculated my sweat rate precisely: 2.8 liters per hour at marathon pace in 90-degree heat. That meant for a 16-mile run at 7:30 pace — roughly two hours — I needed to consume 5.6 liters of fluid. My handheld bottle held 500 milliliters. I was refilling it four times. I needed to refill it eleven times.

I bought a hydration vest. Not a belt — a vest. It holds 2 liters in the back and two 500-milliliter bottles in the front. I mapped out water fountains on the Causeway. I planned my route around refill points. I started pre-hydrating the night before — 500 milliliters of water with a light sodium load before bed, then another 500 when I woke up. I added carbohydrates to my fluids — 6% solution, which aids absorption and provides energy. I stopped relying on thirst and started relying on math.

The next long run was 18 miles. Same heat. Same humidity. Same pace. I finished without incident. My post-run weight was identical to my pre-run weight. My heart rate stayed in zone 3. I did not collapse. I did not see spots. I did not sit on a curb while a tourist took my photo.

I now tell my clients — and my students, and anyone who will listen — that "drink to thirst" is context-dependent. In cool weather, it works. In moderate heat, it is adequate. In Miami in August, it is dangerous. The heat index matters. The sweat rate matters. The duration matters. And if you are not accounting for all three, you are gambling with your health.

The heat wave broke in September. The humidity dropped to a mere 65%. I ran my marathon in October. I finished in 3:14 — not a PR, but a respectable time given the conditions. And I hydrated every step of the way. Not by feel. By data.

I still see that tourist's photo sometimes. It pops up on my Instagram explore page — "Miami locals be like" with a laughing emoji. I do not report it. I deserve it. I was the hydration scientist who forgot to hydrate. And I will never make that mistake again.

— Jordan, from Miami, where the heat is relentless and the water bottles are finally big enough.
