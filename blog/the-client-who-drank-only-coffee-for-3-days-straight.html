The Client Who Drank Only Coffee for 3 Days Straight

By Jordan Lee, RD • September 24, 2025 • 6 min read
I had a client once who told me, with complete seriousness, that he didn’t like water. “It has no taste,” he said. “I only drink coffee.” He was a software developer, late twenties, worked from home, and his daily fluid log looked like a caffeine overdose study. Eight cups of black coffee. Zero cups of water. Zero cups of anything else. He had been doing this for years. He came to see me because he was getting constant headaches, his skin was a mess, and he said he felt “tired all the time but also jittery.” I didn’t know whether to laugh or cry. So I did what I always do. I pulled out the beverage hydration index research. Here’s the short version of that science. The BHI measures how much fluid your body actually retains after drinking a beverage compared to plain water. Water has a BHI of 1.0. Anything above 1.0 is more hydrating than water. Anything below is less. Coffee comes in at around 0.98. That means 1 cup of coffee gives you about 98% of the net fluid of 1 cup of water. The diuretic effect of caffeine is real, but it’s small. For moderate coffee drinkers, you’re not losing net fluid. You’re just not retaining quite as much. But here’s the catch. That 0.98 number assumes moderate intake. One or two cups. Not eight. Not twelve. When you drink eight cups of coffee, the caffeine adds up. Each cup has about 95 milligrams of caffeine. Eight cups is 760 milligrams. That’s a lot. At that level, the diuretic effect becomes more significant. Plus, you’re peeing constantly because caffeine irritates the bladder. My client said he was getting up three or four times a night. That’s not hydration. That’s sleep disruption. So I asked him to do a three‑day fluid log. Every time he drank something, he wrote it down. Also every time he peed, and what color it was. The log was depressing. Day one: coffee at 7 AM, 9 AM, 11 AM, 1 PM, 3 PM, 5 PM, 7 PM, 9 PM. No other fluids. His urine color was dark amber by the afternoon. He had a headache by 2 PM every day. He said he felt “wired but tired,” which is exactly what too much caffeine without enough baseline fluid feels like. Let me do the math on his actual net hydration. Each cup of coffee is about 240 milliliters (8 ounces). Eight cups is 1,920 milliliters (65 ounces). At a BHI of 0.98, the net fluid retention would be about 1,880 milliliters (63.5 ounces) if the diuretic effect scaled linearly. But it doesn’t. The caffeine load changes the renal response. A reasonable estimate for high‑dose caffeine is a net hydration of about 70‑80% of the volume. So his eight cups might have given him only about 1,300‑1,500 milliliters (44‑50 ounces) of net fluid per day. For a 75‑kilogram (165‑pound) man in a temperate climate with a desk job, his baseline need is about 2,500 milliliters (85 ounces) per day. He was running a deficit of about 1 liter (34 ounces) every single day. No wonder he felt terrible. The other problem was his electrolytes. Coffee has almost no sodium, no potassium, no magnesium. He was flushing out what little electrolytes he had without replacing them. Chronic low sodium can cause headaches, fatigue, and brain fog. He had all three. I didn’t tell him to quit coffee. That would never work. I told him to replace two of his eight cups with “the baseline” — plain water — and to add one cup of something with electrolytes, like a sports drink or coconut water. He looked at me like I had suggested he stop breathing. “Two cups less coffee?” he said. “I’ll die.” He didn’t die. He tried it for a week. Two cups of water in the morning before his first coffee. A coconut water in the afternoon instead of his fifth cup of coffee. He came back after seven days looking like a different person. “My headache is gone,” he said. “I slept through the night last night. Like, the whole night.” He was almost crying. The skin thing was the weirdest. He said his face had been red and blotchy for years. He thought it was rosacea. After a week of reducing coffee and adding water, the redness calmed down. Dehydration causes blood vessels to constrict and then dilate irregularly. Chronic dehydration can look like a skin condition. His wasn’t rosacea. It was thirst. We built a new protocol together. Morning: 500 milliliters (17 ounces) of water within 10 minutes of waking. That’s the same rule I use for myself. Then one cup of coffee with breakfast. Then another 500 milliliters of water mid‑morning. Lunch with a glass of iced tea or water. Afternoon: a 16‑ounce (475‑milliliter) electrolyte drink instead of coffee number five. Then one more cup of coffee late afternoon if he really needed it. Evening: only water or herbal tea after 6 PM, so the caffeine didn’t wreck his sleep. His total coffee went from eight cups to three cups. His total fluid went from about 1,900 milliliters (64 ounces) of coffee to about 3,200 milliliters (108 ounces) total — water, coffee, electrolyte drink. That’s a 68% increase in net fluid. His headaches vanished. His skin cleared. His sleep improved. And he still got to drink coffee, which he loves. The beverage hydration index is a useful tool, but it’s not the whole story. BHI tells you the net fluid retention of a single drink in a controlled setting. It doesn’t tell you what happens when you drink eight of them. It doesn’t tell you about electrolyte content or calorie load or bladder irritation. That’s why I use the BHI as a starting point, not an ending point. Here’s the ranking I give my clients. Milk has a BHI of 1.5 — the protein and fat slow gastric emptying, so your body holds onto the fluid longer. Oral rehydration solutions like Pedialyte are about 1.1. Plain water is 1.0. Coffee and tea are about 0.98. Sports drinks are around 0.95 to 1.0 depending on the sugar and sodium content. Juice is around 0.9. Soda is about 0.89. Beer is 0.79. Wine is around 0.7. So if you’re trying to hydrate, milk is technically your best bet. But milk also has calories. A cup of milk has about 150 calories. If you drink four cups of milk a day to hydrate, you’re adding 600 calories. That’s a trade‑off. For most people, the sweet spot is a mix. Plain water for most of your intake — the baseline. Coffee or tea in moderation, one to three cups. An electrolyte drink after sweating. Milk or juice as part of a meal. And limit alcohol and soda because they pull fluid out of you. My coffee‑only client is now a water‑and‑coffee client. He still drinks three cups a day. He says he enjoys them more because they’re not constant. He actually tastes the coffee now instead of just gulping it for the caffeine hit. He sent me a message last week: “I tried a cup of coffee after a 16‑ounce glass of water and it tasted better. Is that real?” Yes, it’s real. Being hydrated improves your sense of taste. I have a tool that helps people figure out their own beverage mix.

 I think the coffee myth — that coffee dehydrates you — is one of the most persistent in all of nutrition. It comes from old studies where they gave people massive doses of caffeine and measured urine output over a few hours. Yes, caffeine is a diuretic. But the net effect of a normal cup of coffee is close to zero. You’re not losing water by drinking it. You’re just not gaining quite as much as if you drank plain water. The bigger issue with coffee is what you’re not drinking. If you replace all your water with coffee, you’re missing out on the electrolyte balance and you’re overloading on caffeine. The same logic applies to tea, soda, and even beer in small amounts. The net hydration of beer is about 79% of the volume. So if you drink a 12‑ounce beer, your body retains about 9.5 ounces of that fluid. The other 2.5 ounces get flushed out through the diuretic effect of alcohol. That doesn’t mean beer is bad. It means beer is not water. For my coffee client, the turning point was the night waking. He had assumed everyone woke up multiple times to pee. He didn’t know that 760 milligrams of caffeine was wrecking his sleep cycle. Once he cut his afternoon and evening coffee, he slept through the night for the first time in years. He told me that alone was worth the change. I see this pattern a lot. People think hydration is just about volume. Drink enough total fluid and you’re fine. But timing matters too. Drinking a liter of water right before bed will make you wake up to pee. Drinking caffeine in the evening will disrupt your sleep architecture even if you don’t notice it. Spacing your fluids throughout the day is almost as important as the total amount. My own rule is no caffeine after 2 PM. I drink two cups of coffee in the morning, sometimes a third before lunch. After that, it’s water, herbal tea, or an electrolyte drink if I’ve worked out. When I stick to that, I sleep like a rock. When I have a late coffee, I wake up at 3 AM and stare at the ceiling. The coffee‑only client is now two years into his new routine. He drinks about 2.5 liters (85 ounces) of total fluid per day, with three cups of coffee and the rest water and the occasional electrolyte drink. His skin is normal. His headaches are gone. He sleeps seven hours without waking up. He says he feels like a different person. I asked him once if he missed drinking only coffee. He laughed. “I miss the idea of it,” he said. “But I don’t miss how I felt.” That’s the best endorsement I could ask for. — Jordan Lee, RD P.S. He still won’t drink coconut water. Says it tastes like “dirty socks.” I told him that’s the potassium. He said he’ll stick to electrolyte tablets. Fair enough.
